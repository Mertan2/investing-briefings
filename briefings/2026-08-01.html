Two things happened on Friday, and they point in opposite directions

This is a weekend edition, so it is worth being blunt about what it is scoring. On Thursday this book wrote that Micron was "the single best-corroborated thesis in the book." On Friday that thesis lost its stop. On Tuesday this book downgraded the war hedge because "the conflict it was armed for has paused." On Friday that conflict reached the Strait of Hormuz. Both moves were made with the information available at the time, and both need adjusting now:

  • The MU base broke, and the reason is structural. Micron fell 5.90% to $823.03. The last briefing's published stop was explicit: "the reclaimed ~$880 base is now well below spot and remains the stop." It is gone. The stock sits roughly 28% below its 25 June 52-week high of $1,255, and the whole memory complex — SanDisk, Western Digital, Seagate, the Korean names — has been dragged into a bear market. The new fact is ChangXin Memory Technologies (CXMT), China's state-backed DRAM maker, which has now gone public, and reports that Apple has sought permission to purchase memory from it. A qualified Chinese entrant at the largest consumer-electronics buyer on earth is a question about Micron's moat. That is a different and more serious category of problem than the cyclical "is the shortage ending" question the market was arguing about a week ago.
  • But the bull case did not disappear, and it is only days old. Samsung warned of memory shortages through 2028. SK Hynix projects memory wafer demand exceeding supply by 20% through 2030. Amazon raised 2026 capex by $20B and named higher memory costs as the cause. And Micron now trades at 5.74× forward earnings with 45 analysts averaging a $1,507.38 target. Both stories are true at the same time. When both are true and the base has broken, the right answer is a smaller position — not a reversed one. Hence Medium, not out.
  • Iran escalated to the chokepoint itself. The Revolutionary Guards said they struck and disabled two oil tankers in the southern Strait of Hormuz on Friday, claiming the vessels were operating under CENTCOM direction and US air escort through a corridor Iran had declared "unsafe and illegal." Iran also halted two vessels attempting to exit. This followed a drone attack on ships at a Mediterranean Egyptian port earlier in the week. Brent rose above $88 and is up more than 20% for the month. Saudi Arabia has now held talks with 43 countries about a maritime coalition to protect shipping.
  • Rates are how an oil shock becomes a portfolio problem. The 10-year Treasury finished Friday at 4.75% — the highest since January 2025 — after breaking above 4.7% on Thursday, driven by oil-fed inflation fear layered on top of the 9–3 FOMC hold with three dissents wanting higher rates. Every long-duration name in this book pays for that: the power sleeve, the renewable income holding, and the AI infrastructure complex all discount off that curve.
  • And the tape ignored all of it. The S&P rose 0.7% to 7,489.72, the Nasdaq 1% to 25,373.85, and the Dow booked a fourth straight winning month — led by Amazon +15.32% to $271.58 as the market decided that AWS accelerating to +37% justified the $220B capex. Of roughly 300 S&P 500 companies reported, 85% have beaten and aggregate profits are tracking above 47% growth. The market is paying up for AI spending with visible revenue behind it, while simultaneously re-pricing the memory that spending consumes. Those two facts are in tension, and next week's SanDisk print is where the tension gets tested.

Why CEG goes out and XOM comes in

CEG out at $262.75 (−0.31%). Constellation was downgraded to Medium on 31 July because it had been re-added on an explicit "calmer oil tape" condition that lasted 48 hours, and because a capital-intensive utility is the wrong thing to own with hikes newly priced. Friday delivered a second and worse leg of exactly that risk: the 10-year at 4.75%, the highest since January 2025. CEG is the most rate-exposed name left in the book, it sits at Medium conviction, and its 52-week range ($228.63–$412.70) shows how far it has already de-rated from the AI-power euphoria. The uncomfortable part, stated plainly: it is being removed three sessions before its own Q2 on 6 August. That is churn, and the previous briefing said the print would be its chance to earn the conviction back. The re-add gate is therefore specific rather than vague — a clean Q2 on 6 August AND a close above $285. If the print is good, it comes straight back.

XOM in at $155.44. This corrects an error this book has already named: on 28 July it sold XOM and RTX on a "catalyst spent" reading of an Iran pause that broke the next day. Friday's print was genuinely mixed — adjusted EPS missed at $3.52 against $3.76 because refinery maintenance prevented it from capturing the stronger fuel margins the oil spike created. Everything underneath is a fortress: revenue $116.0B against $97.81B expected, earnings $14.5B — a four-year high, cash flow from operations $23.6B, free cash flow $17.2B, $9.4B returned to shareholders, upstream adjusted earnings up to $9.2B from $6.3B, and production of 4.5M boe/d on record Permian output above 1.8M boe/d.

The portfolio argument matters more than the earnings one. This book is long AI infrastructure (GEV, VRT, TSM, MSFT), long semis (MU, NVDA, plus INTC and MU in the holdings), and long two rate-sensitive income positions in the portfolio itself. Every one of those is hurt by the same chain: Hormuz tightens → oil rises → inflation expectations rise → yields rise → long-duration equity de-rates. With a 0.16 beta and a 2.65% yield, XOM is the one position that gets paid when that chain runs. It is not a bet that oil goes up; it is insurance priced at a four-year earnings high.

On overriding this book's own gate: the 31 July briefing set "a Brent close above $95 re-arms the full war sleeve." Brent is above $88, not $95, so the gate has not been met — and it is being overridden anyway. The reason is that the $95 level was always a proxy for escalation, and the escalation arrived directly, in a more serious form than a price print: physical interdiction of tankers inside the Strait of Hormuz is a larger event than five dollars of Brent. Naming the override is the point; a gate that gets quietly ignored is worse than one that is publicly revised.
