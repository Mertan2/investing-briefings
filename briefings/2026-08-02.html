What actually changed while the market was shut

Sunday editions have one honest job: not to manufacture activity, but to work out whether the weekend's information changes the probabilities attached to Monday's decisions. This weekend it did, and in the direction opposite to the one Saturday's briefing leaned. Saturday downgraded Micron because its $880 base broke on a story about China's CXMT. Three weekend datapoints attack the severity of that story — not its existence:

  • The single biggest seller was a fund being liquidated, and it has now been taken out of the market. Citadel acquired the majority of the roughly $16 billion public-equity portfolio of Leopold Aschenbrenner's Situational Awareness hedge fund in a privately negotiated block deal, outbidding Millennium and Jane Street, and specifically pre-empting a disorderly open-market fire sale of an AI-heavy book. The reaction was immediate and enormous: Samsung +27%, SK Hynix +30%, the KOSPI up more than 18%, and the Roundhill Memory ETF (DRAM) off its August low of $44.40 to about $49.75–$50.30. Prices that move that far on the removal of a seller were not being set by fundamentals in the first place. This is the most important thing this book learned all weekend, and it reframes rather than reverses the downgrade.
  • SanDisk was sold 12–14% on a DRAM story, and SanDisk does not make DRAM. CXMT is a DRAM manufacturer with no current NAND presence. SanDisk is a pure NAND company. It was nonetheless among the hardest-hit names in the CXMT panic. That is not a market pricing a competitive threat; that is a market selling a ticker because it sits in the same ETF. Meanwhile Goldman Sachs raised its SanDisk price target to $2,200 from $1,200, modelling NAND pricing up 75% to 100%, and noting long-term agreements with a floor of roughly $0.29/GB — broadly in line with current average selling prices, meaning the contracted floor is not a discount to spot.
  • The quantified version of the CXMT threat is much smaller than the narrative version. Mizuho models CXMT's DRAM bit-supply growth at only +13% in 2027 against a 21% industry, and a 2026–2028 CAGR of 24% — in line with the industry, which they characterise as no reckless supply expansion to take share on price. CXMT's global DRAM share was roughly 7.7% in Q4. And the constraint is physical: Applied Materials and Lam Research suspended tool servicing on CXMT's installed equipment in March 2026, much of which is around a decade old and due a refresh it may not be able to buy.
  • The counter-argument is that the leverage has not finished unwinding. Academy Securities is explicit: taking the seller out helped, but "there is so much money in this space that is leveraged … we're going to start seeing some selling pressure again in the next two weeks." Korean margin calls exceeded ₩61bn (~$42M) on Thursday alone, and Samsung plus SK Hynix are 26.4% and 22.7% of the DRAM ETF — so Korean leverage dynamics transmit straight back into the US names. A forced-seller explanation cuts both ways: it means the fall was technical, and it means the technical pressure can return.
  • And on the macro side, OPEC+ met today. The eight core producers convened Sunday 2 August to sign off a +188,000 bpd September quota increase — the fourth identical monthly step and the one that completes the unwind of the 2023 voluntary cuts. More consequential than the number: delegates have indicated the group is preparing to pause quota increases from October 2026 through January 2027. Note the gap between paper and barrels — OPEC+ was producing roughly 7.5 million bpd below its collective target in June because of the conflict. Quota rises that cannot be physically delivered are not bearish for oil, and that asymmetry is what the new XOM position is being paid for.

Response: 0 swaps · 0 downgrades · 0 upgrades. Every live gate in this book is defined on a closing price, and no market closed since the last edition. Manufacturing a swap on a Sunday would be exactly the churn this book criticised itself for on Saturday when it removed CEG three sessions before that company's own earnings. Pre-commitment: what Wednesday 5 August buys or costs

Saturday's downgrade of MU rested on a specific claim: that CXMT is a moat question, not a cycle question. The weekend evidence does not refute that claim, but it materially narrows it. So the response is written down now, before the data, rather than reverse-engineered after it:

  • If SanDisk confirms NAND pricing anywhere near Goldman's +75–100% and guides supply still short — the crash is retrospectively a leveraged unwind wearing a China costume. MU returns to High on the first close above $880, and the deliberately blank add zone re-opens at $820–$870. Not before the close; the gate is the gate.
  • If SanDisk shows NAND pricing rolling over, or hedges its long-term agreements — then the bears were early rather than wrong, the moat question grows a pricing question alongside it, and MU goes to trim-to-core with a hard line at the $800 round number, with the next structural support below that unnamed because there isn't a good one.
  • If the print is strong but the stock sells off anyway — that is the Academy Securities scenario, and it is the one this book will find hardest to trade. In that case the rule is: do nothing for a week. Leverage unwinds are not analysable from public information, and the correct response to a market being driven by margin clerks is to stop having opinions until they finish.

Also recorded: SanDisk's investor day falls on 13 August, eight days after the print, giving management a second date to set long-term targets. A weak print followed by a strong investor day is a real possibility and should not be traded twice.
