The frame gets sharper: it is not spenders vs. recipients, it is funded growth vs. unfunded growth

Tuesday's briefing drew a line between the companies writing the AI checks and the companies receiving them, and bet on the recipients. The week's prints show that line was drawn in roughly the right place but for a slightly wrong reason — and the correction matters, because it changes what to own:

  • Microsoft settled the question in the most emphatic way available. Capex guidance for calendar 2026 held flat at $175B (with ~$50B guided for fiscal Q1 2027), Azure grew +43% — the fastest rate since early 2022 — and management signalled continued cash generation through the new fiscal year. The stock rose 15.63% to $451.58, adding roughly $450B of market value in one session, the largest single-day gain any company has ever posted (past Nvidia's $441B in April 2025), lifting the market cap to ~$3.35T. Alphabet's problem was never that it spent. It was that it spent and its free cash flow went negative. Microsoft spent just as hard and its cash flow did not.
  • Amazon then made the memory trade explicit. AWS revenue $42.23B, +37% YoY against ~31% expected — the fastest growth since 2021 and the fifth straight quarter of acceleration — on group revenue of $200.61B and EPS $5.75. Andy Jassy raised 2026 capex to $220B from $200B and attributed the increase to higher memory costs. That is not a footnote. It is the largest buyer of infrastructure in the world telling the market that DRAM and HBM pricing is now a line item big enough to move a $20B capex revision. Shares rose more than 10% after hours.
  • Meta and Apple show the other side of the same rule. Meta's revenue beat at $60.8B, +28%, but adjusted EPS missed at $6.18 vs $7.13 as AI infrastructure costs compressed margins, and it guided 2026 capex to the top of $130–145B — spend up, earnings down, stock down. Apple posted revenue $109.4B and EPS $2.02, only marginally ahead, with iPhone +21% but services underperforming and no clear AI monetisation story; shares fell ~8% after hours. Same capex regime, opposite verdicts — the difference is whether the revenue line moves.
  • Samsung turned the memory thread into a multi-year one. Samsung reported record Q2 operating profit and sales — and warned of memory shortages through 2028. The memory complex went vertical on Thursday: MU +16% (to ~$896.44, intraday high $911), SanDisk +22%, Western Digital +18%, Seagate +16%, and the Kospi rose 13%, triggering trading curbs. Street price targets on Micron moved to roughly $1,500–$1,700. Combined with Amazon's capex language, this is the single best-corroborated thesis in the book.
  • The Fed turned hawkish, and that is a real cost to the power sleeve. The FOMC held at 3.50–3.75% for a fifth straight meeting, but on a 9–3 vote, with Hammack, Kashkari and Logan all dissenting in favour of higher rates against inflation that has run above target for more than five years. Markets now price two 25bp hikes in 2026 and no movement through 2027. Wednesday was a Fed-fuelled sell-off with a bond rout; Thursday rebounded hard on Microsoft. A rate path that goes up, not down, is a direct valuation hit to leveraged independent power producers and bond-proxy income names.
  • The Iran pause broke in 48 hours — and Tuesday's "catalyst spent" call was early. Trump said the US would hit Iran hard in retaliation for an attempted surprise attack on American forces; strikes resumed. Brent gained 7.9% to $90.74 on Wednesday and touched $91.80 on Thursday before Saudi Arabia proposed a naval coalition to protect Red Sea and Hormuz shipping, knocking it ~2% to $89.03. This book sold XOM and RTX three days ago on the reasoning that the escalation was over. It was not. That is an error worth naming rather than burying.
